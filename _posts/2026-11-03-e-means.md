## It's just faster. By a lot.

Across the datasets tested (real embedding collections, production search indices, synthetic toy data, spanning a few thousand points to ten billion), <span class="e">e</span>-means reaches a loss below Lloyd's full 25-epoch convergence using, on average, 1.2 to 3 epochs. This is not a trade of quality for speed: the loss is lower, not merely close, and it arrives sooner. Measured end to end, that is a 10&ndash;45&times; wall-clock reduction on GPU against a strong k-means implementation, and 8&ndash;15&times; on CPU against Faiss. Speed and quality usually trade against each other in k-means, so in the ordinary sense this shouldn't be possible: close to a free lunch, and the scaling laws below are why it holds.

## What <span class="e">e</span>-means actually is

<span class="e">e</span>-means is a mini-batch update for k-means, but mini-batch k-means already existed. What it changes over earlier mini-batch methods is specific:

- A reliable dead-cluster revival strategy. We tried several and found the one used here, faiss-style splitting of a populous cluster into a dead one, the most effective.
- Two scaling laws that set the batch size and step size automatically, removing the per-dataset tuning earlier mini-batch methods needed.
- Most importantly, the update rule itself. Earlier mini-batch k-means methods (Sculley's, Staleness-Reduction, and others) update each centroid to the plain mean of whatever points land in it this batch. We found an exponential-moving-average update, which blends that batch mean into the centroid rather than replacing it outright, measurably more efficient.
- We also tested updating centroids with standard neural-network optimizers, Adam and SGD, instead of an EMA, and found both consistently less efficient. An EMA update stays, by construction, a weighted mean of the points assigned to it, which is more stable than a gradient step: it cannot overshoot past the data the way a gradient update can.

The EMA update itself is not new on its own; it is also how VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017) updates its codebook, though there it runs inside a neural network on a representation that keeps changing, rather than on a fixed, arbitrary dataset.

Concretely: Lloyd's algorithm assigns every point to its nearest centroid, then sets each centroid to the exact mean of the points assigned to it, one full pass over the data per move. <span class="e">e</span>-means keeps that same mean, but as a running quotient of two exponential moving averages instead of a single exact division: one tracking how many points cluster <span class="e">j</span> has been getting, one tracking their sum.

<div class="eq-display">\[ N_j \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,N_j + \alpha\, n_j, \qquad\quad S_j \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,S_j + \alpha\, s_j \]</div>

where <span class="e">n<sub>j</sub></span> and <span class="e">s<sub>j</sub></span> are the count and the sum of this mini-batch's points landing in cluster <span class="e">j</span> (zero for both if none do). The centroid itself is read off as the ratio:

<div class="eq-display">\[ c_j = S_j / N_j \]</div>

A mini-batch nudges the centroid toward its own mean rather than replacing it outright: the exact amount depends on how much accumulated weight <span class="e">N<sub>j</sub></span> already carries, not a fixed fraction.</figcaption>

Set the batch to the full dataset *and* <span class="e">&alpha;</span> = 1 and <span class="e">N<sub>j</sub></span>, <span class="e">S<sub>j</sub></span> become exactly the count and sum of the whole cluster, so their ratio is exactly one Lloyd iteration. <span class="e">e</span>-means is the same update at a small batch and a small, annealed <span class="e">&alpha;</span> instead: Lloyd is one extreme setting of it, not a different algorithm.

## Why the noisier update wins, not just runs faster

Lloyd can only go downhill, so it has no way out of a bad arrangement once it's in one. A mini-batch update is noisy by construction, and that noise is a small perturbation at every step, the same reason stochastic gradient descent reaches better optima than full-batch gradient descent, not merely a cheaper way to reach the same one.

## Fast kernels alone don't fix this

Flash-KMeans, released by Berkeley in 2026, is a much faster k-means library: better-engineered GPU code, a large real speedup over a naive PyTorch loop and earlier GPU implementations. But it's still full-batch Lloyd underneath, one centroid update per pass over the entire dataset, just computed quickly. That full-batch ceiling is a separate problem from how fast the code runs, and there's a lot of speed left on the table once you fix it: the 10&ndash;45&times; reported above is measured against Flash-KMeans itself, already fast, not against a naive baseline it would be easy to beat.

## The deep learning machinery, precisely

The step size <span class="e">&alpha;</span> is annealed to zero on a cosine schedule rather than held constant, the same curve a learning-rate schedule follows, for the same reason: a step that never shrinks never lets the estimate settle. Dead-cluster revival, a faiss-style rule that replaces a centroid a mini-batch keeps missing by splitting a populous one, is necessary to keep the codebook from collapsing, but not sufficient on its own: without the annealed step and the scaling laws below, reviving the right clusters at the right rate is exactly the per-dataset tuning problem this recipe exists to remove. The last epoch closes the run on its own assignment, each centroid set to the exact mean of the points it received, free, since that assignment was already computed.

What makes this transfer across datasets without tuning is that the batch size `B` and the peak step <span class="e">&alpha;</span><sub>0</sub> are not chosen by hand. Given only `(n, k, T)`, two power laws return both, fit once on one dataset (deep1b) and validated on datasets, scales and dimensions the fit never saw:

<div class="eq-display">\[ B = c_B \, k^{b_k} \, n^{b_n} \, T^{b_T} \]</div>
<div class="eq-display">\[ \alpha_0 = \min\!\Big(c_\alpha \, k^{a_k} \, B^{a_B} \, n^{a_n} \, T^{a_T},\; 1\Big) \]</div>

## The same recipe, from a few thousand points to ten billion

The headline evidence is breadth, not a single favorable number. Across roughly thirty datasets (the 11-dataset VIBE embedding benchmark, production search indices, synthetic toy data, real-world corpora at every scale we could source), the same two scaling laws are applied with zero per-dataset tuning, every time:

- VIBE (11 datasets, 0.2M&ndash;1.5M vectors): 8.5&ndash;21.2&times; GPU and 9.0&ndash;15.0&times; CPU speedup to reach Lloyd@25's loss, in roughly 2 epochs.
- **DINO10B** (10 billion image embeddings, 16 million clusters): below Lloyd's loss in 2.5 epochs versus 25.
- A 13-billion-vector production Instagram index, half a million clusters: matches a custom, hand-built DINO-team codebook in 7 ordinary epochs.
- Verified recently at the small end too: 1,000 and 10,000 points, <span class="e">K</span>&nbsp;=&nbsp;2 and 16. Same recipe, same result, 2 and 4 epochs respectively. This is not a large-scale-only effect.

## In many cases, it beats k-means++ too

A smaller finding, worth stating plainly as secondary rather than the headline: across 40 random restarts per method on several datasets, <span class="e">e</span>-means's *worst* restart beats Lloyd's *best* restart, including when Lloyd is initialized with greedy k-means++, the strongest initialization in common use. This happens because <span class="e">e</span>-means keeps refining the codebook long after a converged Lloyd run has locked in; it is a consequence of the update rule, not something tuned for.
